# Overview of the Constitution of Meridia

Meridia's Constitution has the following major attributes that set it apart from other constitutions and legal systems:

* Defines a government based on economic principles. 
* Has a federal design with a city government and many borough governments within it.
* Strict limitations on borough size and shape, to ensure that "voting with your feet" is easy. 
* Right of secession with certain requirements, so that voting with your feet is not the only way to change jurisdictions.
* The city-level government limited to designating a minimal network of roads and paths, designating nature-space, and settling disputes with borough and city government. 
* Two legislative bodies that both must approve changes to the law: council and the people (via local committees)
* Voting weighted by population vs owned land value vs owned land area must also both pass.
* Limited amount of action council can take per year (in terms of length of time and number of laws) so that the people don't need to spend much of their lives thinking about and considering fighting / supporting legal changes. Also inherently limits the power of politicians and the government.
* Supermajority requirement for laws (60%) so that the legal environment is more stable and to reduce the probability of tyranny of the majority. 
* Statutory law instead of common law to vastly simplify the active legal rules (court precedent does not count as binding law) and eliminate "legislation from the bench" (aka judicial activism) as a means of governance change. 
* Consequentialist outcome-based law. The law may not reward or punish specific methods and means, and must only prescribe outcomes (except in very specific narrow circumstances). This both significantly constrains what law is possible and does so in a way that eliminates most of the kinds of possible bad laws, which either over regulate actions that don't cause harm or create brittle laws that quickly become obsolete.
* Serious consequences for governing officials or agents who violate the constitution. 
* Use of force is equal to all but limited.
* Military and national defense is assumed to be taken care of by a higher government. 
* Borough government limited to defining property rights, promoting efficient competition, taxing or subsidizing externalities, income tax only if necessary, resolving disputes that can't be resolved privately, very limited licensure and information disclosure requirements, UBI and negative income tax as the only welfare options, limited externality-based zoning, and designation of additional travel ways, parks, and nature space.

**Structure**

Constitutions are intended to be labeled with an identifier using numbers identifying the odd sections and capital letters identifying the even sections, like 10, 20.A, 30.B.40, 50.C.60, etc. Numbers are initially spaced 10 apart to leave room for inserts. If so many inserts happen that those numbers run out, lower case letters are intended to be appended to allow additional inserts, where the letters are spaced 10 apart and double or triple letters are used if necessary. Letters can be listed in order normally, but are appended with a number to allow for inserts, with numbers intended to be 10 apart where possible. An example sequence to start might be `10, 20, 20.A, 20.B, 30`, and then with inserts might become `10, 15, 16a, 16e, 16f, 16g, 16h, 16ha, 16he, 16he.A, 16he.B, 16i 16k, 17, 20, 20.A0, 20.A10, 20.A15, 20.A20, 20.B, 30`.

Non-constitutional documents are intended to be numbered using just numbers using the same 10-apart spacing scheme that numbers use for constitutional documents. 

## Freestead Structure and Powers

### Legislative branch

**40. Council:**

* **40.B** City vs rural has for millennia been a contentious struggle, and ensuring that there is a weighting that favors less populated areas prevents the city government from disadvantaging rural areas in favor of dense areas, which allows the city structure to be functional with a far larger jurisdiction than it otherwise would.
* **40.C** This ensures that council members aren't full time politicians, but instead must have non-political occupations most of the year.
*  **40.D** This gives clear incentives for the council to consider the proposals of the local committees.

**50. Local committees:**

* **50.C** This provision gives straightforward incentives to discuss the propositions passed by neighboring committees. 

**60. Council and local committee actions:**

* **60.A** **Vote counting** 
  * **60.A.10** Both council members and citizens must vote on things because elected leaders are in a position to more fully investigate and voice their leadership in a way most citizens can't, elected leaders can be held accountable for constitutional violations in a way citizens can't, but a vote of the citizens ensures that if politicians lie about their preferences, those lies can't translate into adverse policy.
  * **60.A.20** The "limiting vote" only requires one legislative body and only one of the three weighting methods because it is the inverse of a passing vote and will only be used to limit the government.
* **60.B** **Types of laws**. Repealing a normal law (which limits the people) should be easier than creating the law, so that bad laws are removed more often. Repealing a limitation on government should be harder than creating the law, so that limitations on government aren't easily eroded. 60% and 50% are used here so that a 10% swing is necessary to change the law, which increases the stability of laws.
  * **60.B.30** City limitations give the legislature a way to enact limitations to government power that are variably "sticky" and more flexible / less intrusive than a constitutional amendment.
  * **60.B.40** Politicians are limited by the constitution and so have a conflict of interest. Therefore politicians should not be able to block constitutional changes.
  * **60.B.60** The judicial consortium procedure power gives a way to hold the city government to account through a mechanism that isn't entangled with city government officials or bureaucracy, which should eliminate or at least minimize the possibility of a dispute system biased in favor of the city government.
* **60.C** 
  * These word limitations limit how much work the people need to do to evaluate new proposed legislation, reducing information and voting costs to a hopefully manageable level that allow people to mount effective defenses against bad law when necessary.
* **60.D** Public announcement of a proposal and a wait period gives the public time to think about, deliberate on, and mount a defense against the proposal if necessary before it can be enacted.
* **60.E** Successful votes that don't result in full passage of a legislative action expire because opinions among the voting constituency might change after a time. 3 years is chosen because it gives ample time for the other body to deliberate, and some good kinds of change require that kind of time.
* **60.F** Proposals that fail should have a cooldown period so laws aren't just strongarmed through by repeated proposal. 
* **60.G** This prevents the council from simply ignoring the courts when an ambiguity is identified and escalated.
* **60.H** There may be more need of flexibility and touch ups to the law in the beginning as its likely more problems and lessons will come up early on.

**70. Legislative powers:**

* **70.B** The city is intended to set standards for streets and protected space, so the interborough travel standards don't change too quickly as people travel. It does not take part in building or managing them, so that boroughs can innovate on that front. 
* **70.C** Dispute resolution and police
  * **70.C.20** The City police force may be no larger than the largest one employed by a borough so that the city doesn't have overwhelming force. 
* **70.E** City taxes are maximally simple, so that any tax innovation is done at the borough level.
* **70.F** Weapon limitations are there to limit the risk of accidents and negligent use. It's acknowledged that this is unlikely to be effective in preventing the illegal possession of such weapons. 

**75. Administrative powers:**

* These provisions are a replacement for the necessary and proper clause of the US constitution. That clause made the mistake of being overbroad, and also allowing the creation of laws that are "necessary and proper" for carrying out other powers. Instead, the Meridia constitution takes a very narrow approach that allows the power to administer activity but not to make laws on the basis of any administration needs, and does not allow broad rights to argue vague things like whether something was "necessary" or "proper". 

### Judicial branch

**80. Arbitration:**

* **80.C** **Constitutional and ordinance interpretation**. Allowing a defendant to interpret ordinances in any way a most people would interpret it forces the legislature to craft understandable ordinances and gives leniency when ordinances are confusing. Originalist interpretation is used for constitutions so that the meaning doesn't drift over time, like the US constitution has. 
* **80.D** **Jury option**. Judges may sometimes be systemically biased, for example in cases against another arbitration company or in cases where the arbitration company has to prove conflicts of interest. Requiring the option to  have the cased decided by a jury provides the opportunity to sidestep such bias. 12 seems to be a standard jury size and 6 seems to often be a . The fee must in part be paid by the party who requested the jury to ensure all parties have incentives to avoid unnecessary costs.

**90. Freestead arbitration powers:**

* **90.B** A court ordering the legislature to clarify law seems far better than allowing courts or arbitration companies to create law in cases of ambiguity.

* **90.G** The burden of proof should be greater here because the punishment is more costly to society. While in a transfer of money as payment of damages, the receiver of the damage payment gains as much as the payer loses, the same cannot be said for eg prison time. Therefore we should be more sure of these kinds of punishments.

* **90.H** **Cost to the defendant**. 

  * Societal Cost consists of things like the effort and time people must take to be vigilant against harm, preparation to defend themselves, the mental stress involved in that, and opportunity costs for things not done because of the risk of harm from others. The "societal cost" may be included in the estimation of harm done even tho this may sometime or even always result in greater than optimally efficient punishments, because it isn't clear whether or not this is the case. This wiggle room leaves it up to the boroughs to decide what they think is optimal.

  * `L` is given a minimum value if it is lower than 1% because low-value harm that is extremely unlikely to be caught should not place undue punishment on the few people that are caught. There should be a limit to how much a person should be punished for low-harm actions.

  * Regarding `AFF`

    * The plaintiff generally should pay a large fraction of the arbitration fees even in the case they win to disincentivize cases that are much more expensive than the harm caused. It may be reasonable to not charge all the arbitration fees to the plaintiff in cases where the harm done is less than the arbitration fees, because it might result in an increase in significant harm done where defendants can rely on plaintiffs not bringing it to arbitration.
    * The defendant may be ordered to pay some fraction of the arbitration fees to incentivize them not to delay the proceedings and to incentivize them to avoid causing low-value harm.
    * The limitation of arbitration fees the defendant can be ordered to pay relative to the direct harm is so significant low-value harm can be disincentivized without causing very-low-value harm to be severely punished relative to the harm done.

  * Defendants shouldn't have the cost of legal defense included in their fine because the harm / likelihood of getting caught neutralizes the societal cost such that in the average case, the plaintiff's legal costs should be exceeded by the damages. This does mean that it doesn't make economic sense to prosecute every harm for harms that cost more to prosecute than damages received. This makes sense because it would be inefficient to spend more on prosecution than the cost of the original offense.

  * Damage done is the obvious metric by which to decide remuneration of the victim, noting that damage done is not always the same as the market value of an object stolen or destroyed. For example, an old bike might only have $50 of market value, but the work and effort it took to search for a bike and buy it or put together its parts might exceed that by a large margin.

  * Dividing damage done by incidents_caught/estimated_incidence means that types of harm that are caught less often can have a higher punitive fine or sentence to compensate for the fact that the type of harm often goes without restitution. Fining a person more than this would be unfair and not economically justifiable. There are some circumstances where doing harm might be justified if the result is more good is done than harm (for example, breaking into someone's house to put out a fire), however the harm must still be paid for and if harm is unfairly punished, it would cause people to do less good in those cases. Over-punishing crime is a sign that the goal is subjugation and not justice.

    It might be ideal that the fine+sentence always be set to this maximum, since it gives appropriate incentives to private prosecutors to prosecute rarely caught incidents of relatively low-cost harm. The exception might be situations where a permit is requested and steps are taken to assure that the vast majority of instance of harm are recorded so that required compensation can be tallied, in which case only the harm done should be compensated for without any punitive damages. This is a related paper with a similar conclusion: https://www.nber.org/system/files/chapters/c3625/c3625.pdf.

**90.I** **Cost to the Freestead**. 

* It costs money to put people in prison and the benefit is ideally deterrence of future crime. The costs of deterring crime should not exceed the costs of the crime itself. ([see here](http://www.daviddfriedman.com/Academic/Becker_Chapter/Becker_Chapter.html)). 
* `DH + SC - OG` represents the net societal cost of the harm done. For example, if someone steals your bike, that might cost you $100 to buy an equivalent bike + $300 of your time + motivates people in the community to buy more expensive bike locks, raising money spent on bike locks by 0.1% (maybe $40), and the thief could have sold the bike for $50, the net societal cost of the theft would be $440 - $50 = $390. The OG (offender gain) is subtracted because the offender is still part of society. 
* An example to clarify what DE (deterrence effectiveness) is, if there are 100 crimes of this type in a year and the punishment (if applied to all crimes of this type) would reduce 2 crimes, DE (deterrence effectiveness) would be 2% (0.02).  `DE * (DH + SC - OG)` is basically the value of imposing the given sanctions. 
* The purpose of this formula is to neutralize the cost of the sanctions to society. If the costs are high and deterrence is low, then its a net loss to society. 

**90.L** The legal code is set up so that small harms can be pursued but are not monetarily beneficial to pursue. Providing for a way for the arbitration company to dismiss minor cases allows the arbitration company to prevent someone willing to eat the cost from harassing someone else by obtaining disruptive arbitration orders.

**90.M** **Manifest harm and risk imposition**. 

* Risk imposition is a tricky one, because counterfactual risk is much harder to assess than factual events. The manifest harm of risk imposition might be things like creating visibly unsafe conditions that cause others to change their behavior in ways that place costs on them.  
* Harm can include exposure to elevated risk (eg negligence) in lieu of manifest harm because such situations transfer a cost of risk onto other as a negative externality. When the risk imposition is not immediately apparent to the likely victims, it still might be a part of a more general appearance of danger in the kind of activities it is associated with, which has a similar kind of manifest harm where people will incur costs to protect themselves from the risk of this harm. When the risk imposition is not visible at all, not even in a broader statistical sense, it is much less clear what costs this has actually placed on society. 
* One could argue there is a deterrence benefit for that behavior, but economically speaking, the punishments used to deter the kind of activity that causes a risk imposition should come out of the "budget" of total harm that activity does. Meaning, that it seems likely to be optimal that the punishments for those who cause manifest harm because of the risky activity in question should be punished proportionally less when the risk imposition is punished with a significant likelihood.
* The note about the person's mental state is there because it seems to be quite common to assume things about people's mental state when they use drugs (including alcohol) with a lot more confidence than is reasonable, often using statistical averages to make a judgement about an individual that might be an outlier. It is impossible to measure a person's mental state, and thus we should not be making assumptions about their mental state, but should only make determinations based on manifest actions that person does which can allow us to estimate their ability and competency at a given time regardless of mental state. 
* Evidence beyond a reasonable doubt is necessary because likelihoods are much harder to assess than factual events.

**110. Review board:**

**110.A** Selecting at most 40 people for the review board is a trade off between representativeness of the group and how easy it is for the group to discuss and coordinate as well as how costly this is (in terms of people's time).

## 130. Borough powers

* **Levies and externalities**:
  * **130.G** Reimbursing state and federal taxes can reduce deadweight losses without costing the borough much or possibly anything. Theoretically, the reduction of deadweight loss of reimbursed taxes means that the land value will increase for that as well as for the reimbursed amounts themselves. It should be kept in mind that incentives guide people on their margins, and something like a hard cap on the amount of reimbursed taxes would not reduce deadweight loss for anyone who reaches that cap. To reduce the deadweight loss, someone must be able to do more of the taxed activity (earning income, making sales, etc) without being taxed as much as they otherwise would. Although even for people that reach such a cap, it would still attract more people to town, which would be a 3rd way this policy would raise the value of the land. 
* **130.O** Only the borough is allowed to have a welfare program and not the Freestead, so that different boroughs can provide for their own residents their way and so the effects of that program can be compared to adjacent boroughs.
* **130.V** This is allowing the creation of licensing requirements around addictive things and things that have other kinds of negative effects on people. Gambling, drugs, social media use, porn, etc all have effects like this that can distort people's natural responses. This is not intended to prevent anyone from doing these kinds of activities, but instead to ensure they're informed on the dangers, considerations, and proper use of such things. This is here in part because its unclear if such things can be considered externalities, since a person agrees to them, however it seems clear that many people have a propensity to fall prey to their own weaknesses when presented with the opportunity, and education about how those weaknesses operate can lead a person to make better decisions.
* **130.X** A temporary stay on license requirements when fulfilling them isn't available is there so that the borough in question does not use difficult to access license requirements as a backdoor way to ban things. 

## 140. Freestead and borough restrictions

* **140.B** Prohibiting arbitrators from considering government law insulates the Freestead from state and federal power and laws, which may otherwise work very poorly in an environment where laws are enforced with full economic efficiency.
* **140.C** 
  * For example, if a person in borough A creates enough noise to violate borough B's noise ordinance within the borders of borough B, that person can be prosecuted and if brought to Freestead arbitration that prosecution must be upheld. 
  * Violating property rights should be corrected regardless of where the violator was when they acted in a violating manner.
* **140.D** Limiting borough size keeps jurisdictions small and compact, making it easy to vote with your feet. The compactness requirement also limits potential extractive behavior (eg a long thin borough that eliminates LVT to extract land value from across the border). The maximum of 3 miles across is so that every borough will be able to be biked across in 15 minutes. The maximum of 600 acres is because that is the maximum sized area where someone can walk across it in 30 minutes. 
* **140.E** 
  * Laws that ban or require specific procedures and the like become out of date very quickly and result in perpetual suboptimal law and intractability of keeping law up to date. Legislating methods and actions rather than outcomes requires the impossible task of enumerating all the ways bad outcomes might come about, and results in over-broad laws that make harmless or beneficial activity illegal for the purposes of eliminating actions that only sometimes produce bad outcomes.
  * Requiring all ordinances to only legislate on outcomes vastly simplifies what can become an ordinance and goes a long way to make concise legal code of ordinances that are easy to understand, follow, and adjudicate based on. 
  * This also applies to exceptions to rules. Too often our society let's harm be done for things that are "necessary" or "reasonable" when what that means is that certain categories of activity have no incentive to limit the harm they impose on others. For example, many cities allow violations of noise ordinances, even during sleeping hours, for things like garbage trucks, deliveries, emergency vehicles, and especially for anything government employees do. If any "necessary" activity is needs to cause some amount of harm to others and is truly worth that cost, those who are doing it should be willing to compensate those they harm in the process. 
  * The exceptions listed for this are either preventative things (arbitration and security standards, licensing requirements, and admission requirements) or things unrelated to externalities (welfare and tax reimbursement). * **140.G** Rights are explicitly defined in the constitution as limitations of the freestead to make it clear that these are [negative rights](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Negative_and_positive_rights) not positive rights.
* **140.G.60** Votes on moving boroughs or creating new ones are done by land-value and land-area but not residency because the jurisdiction that a property is in should be the sole purview of the land holder, not any renter.

### 150. Limitations on all persons

* Use of force
  * **150.A** This provides specific guidance on what degree of self defense is considered reasonable.
* Security - It is expected that an arbitration proceeding ordering unilateral arrest, therapeutic activity, house arrest, and prison time will not be allowed, and releasing the plaintiff to seek remedy with government police or a government court would be needed for that. It is expected that an arbitration proceeding ordering search, seizure, and rehabilitation training are likely to be limited and that government police with potentially a government court order would need to be sought. It is expected that private security will not be allowed to unilaterally compel someone to stand witness or furnish evidence and that a government court order and government police would need to be used to enforce those things. It is expected that private security performing an arrest, search, or seizure will likely be quite limited and citizen's arrest or government police potentially with a government court order would need to be used to enforce those things. 
  * **150.B** This applies to cases of citizen's arrest, for example. Same goes for **150.C**.
  * **150.D** This is saying that the same requirements in 150.A apply to searching someone, for example if in an altercation someone makes sure the other person has no weapons they could use to escalate the fight.
  * **150.E** This is saying that if a Freestead citizen wants someone searched, they need to first get arbitrators to validate that the situation calls for the use government police.  Same goes for seizure in **150.F**.
  * **150.G** Provides minimum requirements for someone to utilize the police by-right 

### Other discussion

* I considered whether to add provisions about limiting the use of money to promote political campaigns (eg for mayor or council). However, this seems unnecessary since the direct democracy component of legislation creation means that money can't effectively buy politicians that can control a large amount of legislation, and that each piece of legislation would need to be individually promoted in a way that would influence citizens themselves. One could imagine putting a provision in to ban paid promotion of a particular candidate or proposal that is coming to a vote soon, however I'm not sure how to do it in a way that doesn't curtail other kinds of free speech. Everything costs money to distribute, and while perhaps there would be some cost-per-recipient that would be a reasonable line to draw, I don't know what that is. Hopefully the other various limitations on the governance structure are enough to make it not worth the money it would take to corrupt it.
